INDIE FILM AUDIO POST

Looking for a Sound Designer for Your Indie Film?

If you’ve finished shooting an independent film and you’re looking for someone to handle the sound, there’s a good chance you’re searching for a sound designer.

That makes sense. It’s the term most filmmakers know. But it may not actually describe the person you’re looking for.

Job postings for independent films routinely use sound designer to describe a much larger job: clean up and edit the production dialogue, build backgrounds, add sound effects, create Foley, design the more creative elements of the soundtrack, and ultimately mix everything together.

In other words, what many independent filmmakers call sound design is really audio post-production.

What does a sound designer actually do?

In traditional feature-film post-production, sound design is one part of a much larger sound department.

A larger production may have a supervising sound editor, dialogue editors, ADR editors, sound-effects editors, Foley artists and editors, dedicated sound designers, and re-recording mixers. Each person specializes in a particular part of building the soundtrack.

A dedicated sound designer can spend their time creating and manipulating sounds for the moments that actually require design: creatures, weapons, vehicles, technology, dream sequences, subjective perspectives, transitions, or anything else that shouldn’t simply sound like what was recorded on set.

They aren’t necessarily the person cleaning up every line of dialogue or performing the final mix.

That kind of specialization makes sense when a production has the budget and schedule to support a large sound department.

Most independent films don’t.

On an indie film, the jobs get combined

The work doesn’t disappear because the budget is smaller.

Your dialogue still needs to be edited. Production problems still need to be repaired. Scenes need backgrounds. Actions need effects. Missing sounds may need Foley. Certain moments need actual sound design. Music has to be incorporated. Everything eventually needs to become a finished soundtrack.

The difference is that instead of dividing those responsibilities among a large department, an independent feature will usually consolidate them among one person or a small team.

That’s why the credits on independent films can get a little messy.

Someone might begin the project expecting to handle sound effects and end up doing substantial sound design. A re-recording mixer may also edit dialogue. A supervising sound editor may handle effects. Additional editors may be brought in for particular portions of the film. The final credits often reflect the work people actually ended up performing, their level of responsibility, and the credits negotiated by the team.

The important question for the filmmaker isn’t really “What is this person’s title?”

It’s:

“Can this person or team take the sound I have now and turn it into a finished movie?”

Why searching for a “sound designer” can be confusing

There’s another complication: sound designer has become an extremely broad title.

It is heavily used in video games and interactive media, where sound designers may specialize in creating assets and implementing them into game engines. It’s also used in theater, podcasts, installations, music, advertising, and other forms of media.

Those are legitimate sound-design disciplines, but they’re not necessarily film audio post-production.

Meanwhile, many experienced film-sound professionals describe themselves using titles such as re-recording mixer, supervising sound editor, sound editor, or audio post-production engineer.

So it’s entirely possible to search for a “sound designer,” find someone with an impressive portfolio, and discover that they aren’t actually equipped to take a feature film from production audio through final delivery.

It’s also possible to miss exactly the person you’re looking for because their website says Re-Recording Mixer rather than Sound Designer.

What should an independent filmmaker actually look for?

If you need someone to finish the entire soundtrack, look for a person or team experienced specifically in film audio post-production.

Depending on your film, that process can include:

  • Production dialogue editing and cleanup
  • ADR editing and integration
  • Backgrounds and ambiences
  • Sound-effects editing
  • Foley
  • Creative sound design
  • Music editing and integration
  • Re-recording mixing
  • Stereo and surround versions
  • Stems, M&E tracks, and other distributor deliverables

You may not need every item on that list. But whoever is leading your sound post should understand how all of those pieces fit together.

That’s particularly important if your film may eventually go beyond festivals and require professional distribution deliverables.

You probably don’t need a Hollywood-sized sound department

The traditional feature-film sound workflow exists for good reasons. Specialization lets highly experienced people concentrate on what they do best, and a large team can accomplish an enormous amount of detailed work on a demanding schedule.

But an independent filmmaker usually can’t hire a dozen people to finish the soundtrack.

Fortunately, you usually don’t have to.

A smaller, experienced audio-post team can use many of the same techniques and workflows while combining roles intelligently. The goal isn’t to imitate the staffing of a major studio production. It’s to understand which parts of that process matter to your film and scale the workflow appropriately.

That’s how I approach independent features at BDS.

I work as a re-recording mixer and sound designer, but on an independent film my job is often considerably broader than either title suggests. Depending on the project, BDS can take responsibility for the complete soundtrack—from dialogue editorial through sound effects and design to the final mix and delivery.

Additional editors can be brought in where the schedule or complexity of the film makes specialization useful. Other projects are better served by keeping the team extremely small.

The workflow should fit the movie rather than forcing the movie into a predetermined post-production structure.

Remote sound post has changed the equation

None of this necessarily requires the filmmaker and sound team to be in the same city.

Most of the editorial and mixing process can be completed remotely. Picture and production audio are turned over digitally, the soundtrack is built and mixed, review versions are provided to the filmmakers, and notes are incorporated into subsequent passes.

For many independent filmmakers, that notes-based process is actually more practical than sitting through days of attended mixing.

When a director, producer, distributor, or streaming platform does require an attended final mix, that can be handled remotely in real time or in an appropriate mixing facility.

That means an independent filmmaker in Washington, Georgia, Texas, or anywhere else doesn’t necessarily need to find the best available sound person within driving distance.

You can choose the sound team that makes sense for the film.

So, yes—you may need a sound designer

There may be sequences in your movie that genuinely need creative sound design.

But if what you really mean is:

“I’ve finished my film. Now I need someone to make it sound like a finished movie.”

You’re looking for something broader.

You’re looking for film audio post-production.

And whether the person you hire calls themselves a sound designer, supervising sound editor, audio post-production engineer, or re-recording mixer matters considerably less than whether they understand the entire process required to get your film from production audio to a finished soundtrack.
