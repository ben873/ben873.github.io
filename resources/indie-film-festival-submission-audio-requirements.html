FILM FESTIVAL SOUND GUIDE

How Do I Prepare My Indie Film’s Sound for Festival Submissions?

If you're finishing an independent feature and preparing for festival submissions, one of the most confusing questions is:

How finished does the sound need to be before I submit?

The answer depends on the festival.

Some major festivals accept works in progress with temporary or incomplete sound. Some allow you to update the screener later. Others only review the version you initially submit.

And there is another important distinction:

The audio requirements for submitting a film are not necessarily the same as the audio requirements for exhibiting it if you're selected.

That matters because a stereo submission may be perfectly acceptable for consideration while the eventual theatrical presentation may require or strongly prefer a surround master.

If your film may ultimately need 5.1, it's worth planning for that before the festival acceptance email arrives.

Major festival sound requirements

Festival specifications change from year to year, so always confirm the current rules directly with the festival before creating final deliverables.

But the differences between the major festivals illustrate why it's useful to plan your sound-post workflow early.

The important lesson isn't that every festival requires 5.1.

They don't.

The lesson is that a festival submission and a festival exhibition master are two different stages of post-production.

You may not need a finished surround mix to submit

If a festival accepts works in progress, you may be able to submit before the entire sound-post process is complete.

That can be useful when a submission deadline arrives before the film's actual finishing schedule.

A submission version might contain:

  • edited and intelligible dialogue
  • important sound effects
  • major creative sound-design moments
  • temporary or partially completed backgrounds
  • music
  • a strong stereo review mix

That may be enough for programmers to properly experience the film.

Meanwhile, sound post can continue.

The mistake is assuming that because a stereo screener was acceptable for submission, the soundtrack is therefore finished.

Those are two different questions.

A stereo submission can still be part of a surround workflow

Suppose you're preparing a feature for Sundance.

You don't necessarily need to rush through the entire final 5.1 mix simply because the submission deadline is approaching.

You might submit a strong stereo version while continuing to build the full soundtrack.

The sound-post process can then continue through:

  • dialogue editorial
  • ADR where necessary
  • backgrounds
  • sound-effects editorial
  • Foley
  • sound design
  • surround preparation
  • final 5.1 re-recording mix
  • stereo version
  • stems and other deliverables

That can be a perfectly sensible schedule.

What you don't want is to reach selection and discover that the existing sound-effects edit was never constructed in a way that supports the surround mix you're now required to deliver.

You can't create a surround soundscape at the DCP stage

This is an important distinction.

Creating a 5.1 DCP is not the same thing as creating a 5.1 soundtrack.

If your picture edit contains one stereo background for each scene, you can technically route that material into a surround environment.

But that does not necessarily create a properly built surround soundscape.

A residential exterior may need separate traffic, birds, wind, neighborhood activity or other elements that can be distributed around the audience.

An interior restaurant may need independent crowd, kitchen, dish, HVAC and exterior elements.

A vehicle that needs to move through the theater needs to exist independently rather than being embedded inside a stereo ambience.

Those decisions begin during sound-effects editorial and sound design, not during DCP creation.

So if a festival's eventual exhibition format requires or strongly favors surround, the best time to think about surround is during the sound edit.

Not after selection.

Work-in-progress submissions can be very useful

Submitting a WIP is not inherently a compromise.

It can be a strategic way to keep the festival calendar moving while post-production continues.

The important question is whether the version being submitted represents the film well enough to be judged.

If the dialogue is difficult to understand, a key emotional scene has no sound design, or music transitions are unfinished enough to distract from the story, submitting early can hurt the film.

But if the major creative work is in place and the remaining work is refinement, additional editorial and final mixing, a WIP may represent the movie perfectly well.

Different festivals handle this differently.

Read the replacement rules carefully

This is one of the most important things to check before submitting a WIP.

Some festivals allow updated screeners.

Others don't.

At Tribeca, for example, a filmmaker may be able to update the same screener during the submission period.

At SXSW, the first submitted version is the version programmers evaluate.

Cannes likewise warns filmmakers that submitting a working copy does not mean they can later replace it with the finished version for preselection.

That changes the decision.

If the festival only reviews one version, the question becomes:

Is this cut good enough to represent the film now?

If updates are permitted, you may have more flexibility to continue improving the film during the submission window.

Sound post doesn't have to stop while you're submitting

A common misconception is that festival submission creates a hard finishing boundary.

It doesn't necessarily.

You can submit one version while continuing to work on another.

For example:

Festival Cut A

  • picture substantially locked
  • dialogue cleaned and edited
  • important effects and design in place
  • stereo review mix
  • submitted to festival

Meanwhile:

Sound Post Continues

  • detailed dialogue editorial
  • complete backgrounds
  • Foley
  • additional sound design
  • surround preparation

Then:

Final Cut B

  • picture revisions incorporated
  • sound session reconformed
  • final 5.1 mix
  • final stereo
  • stems
  • DCP / exhibition deliverables

This kind of workflow is very common when festival deadlines arrive before the true end of post-production.

What if picture changes after sound post has started?

This is where professional reconforming becomes valuable.

Suppose you submit your film and then decide to shorten a scene, replace a shot or restructure part of the ending.

The sound editorial already completed against the previous picture does not necessarily need to be rebuilt from scratch.

If the turnover has been prepared correctly, the old and new picture edits can be compared and the existing sound session can be reconformed to the revised cut.

This is one reason I request video EDLs at picture turnover.

They give the sound department a machine-readable map of the edit.

If a revised picture arrives, we can identify what moved, what changed and what stayed the same, then move the existing sound work accordingly.

Picture changes still create work.

But they're very different from manually rebuilding the soundtrack every time the filmmaker exports a new cut.

Keep sound and picture versioning organized

If you're submitting while post continues, clear versioning becomes extremely important.

Don't call every export:

FINAL.mov

Use explicit version names.

For example:

  • Feature_FestivalCut_v01
  • Feature_FestivalCut_v02
  • Feature_PictureLock_v03
  • Feature_FinalMix_v04

The exact naming convention matters less than everyone knowing which picture version the sound session currently matches.

Whenever picture changes, the sound team should receive:

  • the new reference picture
  • new video EDLs
  • any updated audio turnover material if necessary
  • clear notes identifying major changes

That makes continuing sound post much safer.

Should I finish sound before submitting?

There isn't one answer.

It depends on three things:

1. How finished does the submission need to feel?

Festival programmers are evaluating the film, not grading your post-production paperwork.

But sound has an enormous effect on how professional and emotionally effective a film feels.

If unfinished sound is distracting from the movie, keep working.

2. Does the festival allow a WIP?

Read the rules carefully.

Some explicitly accept unfinished work.

Others may accept it technically but only review the first version submitted.

3. What will you need if you're selected?

This is where filmmakers sometimes plan too narrowly.

You may only need a stereo screener today.

But if your likely exhibition path includes a festival with surround theatrical requirements, you should structure the sound post so that reaching 5.1 later is part of the plan rather than an emergency.

If you're targeting Sundance, plan for surround before acceptance

Sundance is a useful example because its published theatrical technical specifications explicitly call for a 5.1 surround DCP.

That does not mean the festival submission screener needs to be a completed 5.1 master.

It means that if Sundance is a serious target, the sound-post schedule should already account for eventually producing one.

The difference is important.

You don't necessarily need to finish 5.1 before submitting.

But you should avoid making editorial decisions that make building 5.1 unnecessarily difficult later.

Venice makes the same point in a different way

Venice explicitly prefers 5.1 or 7.1 exhibition material and discourages stereo playback in large theaters.

That is useful information even if your submission screener is stereo.

It tells you something about the environment in which the film may eventually be experienced.

A theatrical soundtrack is not simply a louder version of the review mix.

It has to work in a room.

What if I don't know which festivals I'll get into?

That's normal.

Most independent filmmakers don't know their exact final exhibition requirements while sound post is beginning.

The solution isn't to build every possible deliverable immediately.

It's to organize the project in a way that preserves options.

That means:

  • retain the original production sound
  • preserve multichannel source recordings
  • keep effects separated when useful
  • build enough independent backgrounds for surround
  • maintain dialogue, music and effects stems
  • keep clear picture-version records
  • preserve the ability to reconform
  • avoid unnecessary flattening of material

A well-organized sound session makes future deliverables much easier.

The festival strategy and sound-post strategy should support each other

The most efficient workflow is usually not:

finish everything → submit → hope

and it's also not:

submit anything → worry about sound if accepted

A better approach is to understand the festival calendar and plan post around it.

Maybe the submission deadline becomes the target for a strong stereo WIP.

Maybe detailed Foley continues afterward.

Maybe picture continues changing while dialogue editorial begins.

Maybe the final surround mix happens once the filmmakers are truly done adjusting the cut.

Those decisions can be made intelligently if everyone knows the plan.

At BDS, I plan for both the submission and the final film

When I'm working on an independent feature with festival deadlines, I don't automatically treat the first submission file as the end of sound post.

Sometimes the right strategy is to finish the entire soundtrack before submission.

Other times it makes more sense to create a strong submission version and continue toward the final theatrical soundtrack afterward.

If picture changes along the way, the sound session can be reconformed rather than rebuilt.

If the eventual exhibition requires surround, the sound-effects edit can be built with that destination in mind from the beginning.

The exact workflow depends on the film, the deadline and the festivals being targeted.

The important thing is understanding that:

submission sound, final sound and exhibition deliverables are related stages of the same process—not necessarily the same file.

The short version

If you're preparing an independent feature for festivals:

You may not need a finished 5.1 mix before submitting.

You do need a submission version that represents the film well.

You need to understand whether the festival will allow updated cuts.

And if the festival may ultimately require or strongly prefer surround exhibition, you should make sure the sound editorial you're doing now will support that final mix later.

That way, getting selected creates a delivery deadline.

Not a sound-post emergency.
