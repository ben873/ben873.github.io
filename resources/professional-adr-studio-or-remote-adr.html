ADR FOR INDEPENDENT FILMS

Do I Need a Professional ADR Studio for My Independent Feature?

ADR can be one of the more expensive parts of sound post-production, but not every production needs a traditional ADR session at a professional facility.

A feature with hundreds of replacement lines, multiple actors, complicated performance requirements or a tight turnaround can benefit enormously from a dedicated ADR stage. The room, engineer, recording setup and workflow allow a large number of cues to be recorded quickly and consistently.

Many independent features have much smaller ADR requirements.

After dialogue editing and restoration, a production may only have a handful of lines that truly need to be replaced. There may also be added lines, off-screen dialogue or phone conversations that never existed in the production recordings.

For those productions, booking a professional ADR facility for every actor and every cue may not be the best use of the sound budget.

A remotely supervised ADR session can often provide what the production needs with a relatively simple recording setup. The important part is understanding which cues need to match production dialogue closely, which can tolerate a simpler recording environment, and when the amount or complexity of the work makes a professional ADR facility worthwhile.

When a Professional ADR Studio Makes Sense

The biggest advantage of a professional ADR facility is efficiency.

A production with hundreds of cues across several actors needs to move through those cues quickly. A dedicated ADR room provides a controlled acoustic environment, appropriate microphones, picture playback, cueing, recording and an engineer who can concentrate on keeping the session moving.

That becomes increasingly valuable as the scope grows.

A few replacement lines for one actor can be relatively straightforward. Hundreds of cues involving multiple actors, different scenes, different microphone perspectives and complicated performances are a different type of production problem.

Professional facilities are also useful for physically demanding performances, screaming, whispering, efforts, singing, group ADR and scenes where the actor needs room to move while performing.

Schedule matters as well. If ADR needs to be completed quickly and immediately turned over to dialogue editorial and the mix, a dedicated ADR workflow can save a significant amount of time.

For a production with extensive ADR requirements and the budget to support them, a professional ADR studio is usually money well spent.

Most Independent Features Need Much Less ADR

The amount of ADR a production actually requires isn’t always obvious until dialogue editorial has been completed.

A noisy production recording doesn’t automatically need to be replaced.

Dialogue editing, alternate microphone channels, production takes and modern restoration tools can often preserve recordings that initially appear unusable. Once that work is complete, the ADR list may be considerably shorter than expected.

The remaining cues tend to be the lines where production audio genuinely can’t be recovered or where the performance, edit or story has changed.

A line may have been destroyed by traffic or an aircraft. A microphone may have clipped. Clothing noise may cover an important word. An edit may require dialogue that was never recorded. The director may simply want to change a line after picture has been shot.

When the final list consists of a relatively small number of cues, the economics of ADR change considerably.

Remote ADR Can Be a Practical Alternative

For limited ADR, the actor doesn’t necessarily need to travel to a commercial recording facility.

The re-recording mixer or dialogue editor can supervise the session remotely while the actor records from another location.

This is very different from asking an actor to record lines alone and email the files afterward.

The mixer can listen to the recording environment, help position the microphone, establish appropriate recording levels and work through each cue with the actor.

More importantly, the mixer can evaluate the performance against picture.

ADR needs to match more than the words. Timing, pacing, energy, projection, breathing and physical effort all contribute to whether the replacement recording will actually work in the scene.

A remotely supervised session preserves much of that professional oversight without requiring the production to book a commercial ADR stage.

Building a Temporary ADR Recording Space

A useful remote ADR setup doesn’t necessarily require building a recording studio.

It does require controlling the room.

Normal bedrooms and living rooms can create obvious reflections that become part of the recording. Those reflections can make ADR difficult to integrate with production dialogue later.

A closet full of hanging clothes can be surprisingly effective because the clothing absorbs sound.

Couch cushions, heavy blankets, comforters and other absorptive materials can be positioned around the actor to reduce additional reflections. The objective isn’t to create a beautiful permanent studio. It’s to temporarily create a small, quiet recording area without an obvious acoustic signature.

The recording space also needs to be evaluated for noise. Air conditioners, refrigerators, computer fans, street traffic and other household sounds can become very noticeable on a close microphone.

A quiet closet with improvised acoustic treatment can produce a more useful ADR recording than an expensive microphone placed in a large reflective room.

A Basic Remote ADR Recording Setup

The equipment requirements can be fairly modest.

A useful setup consists of a computer, headphones, an audio interface and an appropriate microphone.

If production microphones are available, they can be particularly useful.

When possible, recording both a boom and lav can provide valuable options during dialogue editorial. The boom may provide the primary ADR recording while the lav gives the editor another perspective that may match the surrounding production dialogue more naturally.

Using the exact production microphone can help, but microphone model alone doesn’t determine whether ADR will match.

Distance, perspective, room acoustics, performance and the production ambience surrounding the dialogue are all important.

The objective is to give the dialogue editor and mixer recordings that can be convincingly integrated with the material around them.

Not Every ADR Cue Needs the Same Recording Quality

This is an important distinction when deciding how much to spend on ADR.

A replacement line that needs to cut seamlessly between two pieces of clean production dialogue deserves considerable attention to recording quality, microphone perspective and room acoustics.

The audience shouldn’t hear the transition.

Other dialogue can be much more forgiving.

A phone conversation is a good example.

If new dialogue will ultimately be futzed to sound like it’s coming through a phone, radio, television, intercom or similar source, the recording will intentionally be filtered and processed during the mix.

Perfectly matching the production microphone may provide very little benefit in that situation.

The performance still matters. The recording still needs to be clean enough to work with. But a simple remote setup may be completely appropriate for material that will ultimately be bandwidth-limited, distorted, compressed or otherwise heavily processed.

Added off-screen dialogue, background voices and announcements can sometimes fall into the same category.

ADR should therefore be evaluated based on how the recording will actually be heard in the finished production.

One cue may deserve a professional ADR room and careful microphone matching. Another may work perfectly well from a remotely supervised home setup. A futzed phone voice may require little more than a clean performance recorded without excessive noise or room reflection.

Treating all three recordings as though they have identical requirements can add expense without meaningfully improving the finished soundtrack.

Remote ADR Software

The internet connection between the actor and mixer is another part of the workflow.

Source-Connect is widely used for professional remote audio recording and is generally a strong choice for ADR. It is designed specifically for high-quality remote recording workflows and provides tools that make working across an internet connection considerably more practical than relying on a conventional video call.

SessionLinkPRO is another option. It provides low-latency, high-quality audio through a browser and can be useful when installing dedicated software at the actor’s location isn’t desirable.

Cleanfeed provides another browser-based approach to high-quality remote audio and can work well for relatively simple recording setups.

The exact platform can vary depending on the production, actor and equipment available.

What’s important is maintaining reliable communication with the actor while capturing a high-quality isolated recording for editorial.

A conventional Zoom or FaceTime connection can still be useful for communication or picture reference, but the heavily compressed audio from a normal video call generally shouldn’t be treated as the final ADR recording.

Local Recording Provides Another Layer of Security

When practical, the actor’s system can also record the microphone locally.

This creates an original recording that hasn’t traveled across the internet.

The remote connection can be used for monitoring, communication and directing the session while the local recording provides another source for the final dialogue editorial.

After the session, the original files can be transferred to the mixer and aligned with the selected performances.

For an independent production, this can provide a very robust workflow without requiring an elaborate remote studio.

Performance Is More Important Than Expensive Equipment

One of the easiest ADR mistakes is concentrating on recording technology while ignoring performance.

A beautifully recorded line that doesn’t match the original performance won’t work.

An actor who was running, fighting, crying or speaking across a room during production may sound completely different when standing still in a closet several months later.

Projection changes. Breathing changes. Rhythm changes.

Sometimes the actor may need to recreate some of the physicality of the original scene while recording. Even small movements or changes in posture can make a performance feel considerably more natural.

Perspective matters as well.

Dialogue recorded for a close-up shouldn’t necessarily sound identical to dialogue intended for a wide shot. Microphone distance and performance can be adjusted to give the dialogue editor material that better represents the perspective seen on screen.

This is one of the advantages of having the person responsible for dialogue editorial or the final mix supervise the ADR session. They’re not simply trying to capture a technically clean recording. They’re listening for a recording that can actually be integrated into the production.

Where Remote ADR Can Save an Independent Production Money

The savings from remote ADR extend beyond the cost of the recording room.

A traditional session can involve facility time, an engineer and scheduling costs. There may also be transportation and travel expenses, particularly once principal photography has ended and the cast is no longer in the same location.

An actor who now lives across the country may only have four lines that need to be replaced.

Flying that actor back, booking a facility and assembling a traditional ADR session can cost considerably more than the actual dialogue work requires.

A remote session allows the production to spend money where professional expertise is most useful: preparing the cues, supervising the performance, editing the recordings and integrating them into the final mix.

When Remote ADR Stops Making Sense

Remote ADR works best when the scope is manageable.

As the cue count increases, the efficiency of a dedicated ADR facility becomes more valuable. The same is true when many actors need to be recorded, performances are complicated, the schedule is compressed or the production requires group ADR and other specialized recording.

There is also a practical limit to what can be accomplished in someone’s home.

Some locations are simply too noisy. Some rooms can’t be sufficiently controlled. Some actors won’t have access to the necessary equipment or won’t be comfortable operating it.

At that point, the effort required to avoid a professional facility can cost more time than it saves.

The purpose of remote ADR isn’t to replace professional ADR studios.

It’s to avoid using a large-scale solution for a small-scale problem.

Choosing the Right ADR Workflow

ADR should be approached cue by cue and production by production.

A feature with hundreds of cues, multiple actors and a tight delivery schedule may benefit enormously from a professional ADR facility.

A feature with twelve replacement lines may not.

Even within the same production, different cues can justify different recording approaches. A critical replacement line surrounded by clean production dialogue may deserve a carefully controlled recording setup. A new phone line that’s going to be heavily futzed may be recorded successfully with something much simpler.

The appropriate workflow is the one that produces material suitable for its final use.

For independent filmmakers, that can mean combining professional dialogue editorial with remotely supervised recording, simple temporary acoustic treatment and readily available recording equipment.

The goal isn’t to make ADR as inexpensive as possible.

It’s to avoid spending money on recording quality and infrastructure that won’t provide a meaningful benefit once the dialogue reaches the finished mix.
